I felt intense joy when the soccer World Cup final between Argentina and France culminated in a triumph for Lionel Messi. I, probably along with [many others](https://www.espn.com/soccer/fifa-world-cup/story/4835721/the-world-cup-belongs-to-lionel-messi), desperately wanted Messi to win this World Cup. “Wait,” you will ask, “how can you say this?”

“You were born in Amsterdam, the Netherlands,” you might say. Did I _not_ witness the defeat of the Netherlands at the hands of Argentina in the quarterfinal match at this very same 2022 World Cup? Do I _not_ know about the taunts some of the Argentina players directed at the Dutch players after the penalty shootout? Do I _not_ know about the insult Messi himself threw at one of the Dutch players after the game? Did I not _proudly_ wear an orange soccer shirt for the Netherlands vs USA round of 16 game, which was [won by the Dutch team](https://www.theguardian.com/football/2022/dec/03/netherlands-usa-world-cup-last-16-match-report)? And, going back further, do I not _still_ feel the national pain of the loss against Argentina in the 1978 soccer World Cup finals?

Yes, yes, I do know, I did experience these things, and, of course, I am Dutch. So let me explain.

The story of Lionel Messi is well known but here are the main points. Messi came to Barcelona, Spain, at a very young age, played soccer at FC Barcelona for many years, won basically everything there is to win in the Spanish club league and [European soccer leagues](https://www.uefa.com/uefachampionsleague/news/0242-0e97e0ac1cb3-eef786ff788c-1000--lionel-messi-what-records-does-he-hold/), many times over. He won the World Player of the Year award, a.k.a. the [Ballon d’Or](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballon_d%27Or), seven times and holds an insane number of other [records](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_career_achievements_by_Lionel_Messi) in the world of soccer.

Except, he never won a World Cup with his national Argentina team, at least until this year. Messi, although recognized as being great, endured years of living in the shadow of Diego Maradona, another one of soccer’s legendary players. Maradona _did_ win a World Cup for Argentina, in the 1986 tournament.

For years, people brought up the question of whether Messi could be considered the _greatest of all time_, not having won the World Cup with his national team. The player that is oftentimes considered the greatest player ever, [Pelé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pel%C3%A9), won the World Cup three times with Brazilian teams.

Messi was criticized in the Argentina media for not being able to bring the World Cup home, while winning so many trophies with his Barcelona club team in Europe. Never mind that soccer is a team sport, with 11 players on the field, plus substitutes, coaches, medical staff, etc. A single player can never win a match by him- or herself in soccer, no matter how great their talent. At 35, this was surely Messi’s last opportunity to win a World Cup tournament.

I grew up playing soccer on the streets of Amsterdam as a kid and played indoor soccer as an adult. I like to think this gave me a heightened sense of appreciation for the amazing soccer techniques demonstrated by Messi. The creativity and intelligence are superb. For me, no other soccer player embodies the joy and beauty of the game in quite the same way.

Even though Messi can be ruthless on the field, punishing even the slightest misstep or hesitation of his opponents, his personality off the field was seen as boring and bland. Whereas Maradona was brash, outspoken, and brazen, Messi is quiet and reserved off the field. He lives a private and seemingly happy family life in Barcelona with his wife, kids, and parents. Messi doesn’t live a life of decadence.

Messi is said to be an [introvert](https://www.offtheball.com/soccer/inside-the-life-of-lionel-messi-why-he-is-so-difficult-to-coach-1066730), perhaps feels he is not as articulate as others, and doesn’t desire to be seen as a prominent spokesman for his club or team. He would rather simply play soccer and show his passion and determination on the field. He would rather display his soccer intelligence through his actions in the game. He is [said to appreciate](https://www.nytimes.com/2022/12/17/world/americas/world-cup-final-messi-argentina.html) the fact that his journey was a long one and not easy. In fact, his pain and suffering for missing the ultimate prize in international soccer seemed to be visible through his body language in some of his games with the Argentine team.

For me, personally, it’s not the impressive records, it’s the why and how. And this is what I mean by saying that it felt this win was for all of us. For all of “us”, who feel they are a bit on the introvert side, who don’t like to talk big, who don’t like cheating their way to victory, who feel they just want to play the game, do the work and do it well. For those of “us” who feel they are on a path that may feel long and difficult at times, acknowledging mistakes and enduring criticism along the way, not knowing where the path may lead or whether there even is a path. For those who don’t have an impervious self-confidence or unshakeable belief that everything will be all right with the world. For a few moments there, at the end of that finals game, it seemed like things are going exactly the way they should. The stories continue, but this chapter came to a wonderful end. This was a beautiful gift.

To the French team fans and other Dutch soccer fans that feel very differently, I understand and apologize, this piece is not for you. But I’m curious about others that felt the same way I did, please let me know!
